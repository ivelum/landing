Every so often, someone in a startup chat asks how to find a technical
cofounder. Some argue you don't need one at all and should just vibe-code the
prototype yourself. Others share tips for interviewing technical cofounders.
So let's look at the options, with the pros and cons of each.


## Just vibe-code it!

This is a popular piece of advice online: why spend time, money, and effort
convincing someone to build it for you when you can build it yourself with AI?
There are caveats (more on those below), but if you haven't tried it yet,
my advice is—go for it! Seriously. You might end up with a decent proof of
concept and some early user feedback. You can certainly build a landing page,
hook up Stripe, and who knows—maybe land your first paying customers.

There are still good reasons to bring a technical co-founder onto the team
later. But even then, these skills stay useful for prototyping and
communicating your ideas. Showing someone an AI-generated prototype is often
faster and easier than describing the concept in words.


## Caveats

We recently surveyed tech leaders about how they use AI and what worries them
most. You can
[read the full report here](/blog/ai-security-survey-2026), but the one-line
summary is that everyone is concerned about quality and security. Looking
specifically at the startup scene, I'd add a third concern of my own: a lack
of taste.

In 2026, the world is full of half-baked, vibe-coded MVPs that nobody needs.
Claude-generated landing pages all look alike and inspire little trust. When
one of these MVPs fails to gain traction, many founders wrongly conclude that
the idea was flawed and move on, when the real problem was execution. The last
thing anyone needs is another piece of AI slop.

Producing quality work with AI requires a human in the loop. If you don't give
the model specific instructions, it will make its own decisions, and those
decisions are hit or miss. The more you build on AI judgment alone, the more
cracks appear in the product. You add a feature and something else breaks.
Some features are working very slowly, but you don’t know why. A customer
reports corrupted data, but you’re not sure what happened. Someone asks,
"Is it secure?" and you don't know what to say, because you genuinely don't
know.

I'm not saying this to discourage you from using AI — quite the opposite.
Try it, and learn where its limits are, so you have a clearer sense of what's
possible and how to use it well.


## Do you need a technical cofounder?

Let's start with a slightly different question: if you're building a technology
startup, is it useful to have someone on the team who actually understands
technology? I'd say yes — absolutely, no doubt about it.

Think about the competition. If you're a non-technical founder building your
product through vibe-coding while your competitors have hired professional
developers, who has the edge? Developers use AI too, but they operate on a
different level: they can see what's happening under the hood. They can guide
it better, anticipate the consequences of specific technical decisions, triage
problems far faster, hand-code the parts where AI falls short, and take much
better care of product quality, reliability, and security.

Maybe that person doesn't have to be a cofounder. Maybe they don't have to
join on day one. But if you're serious about your startup, I'd argue you need
someone technical on board eventually.

## Technical cofounder - traits to look for

First and foremost, the two of you have to get along well. You’re going to work
together for years in a highly stressful environment, so this matters
enormously. Someone can be extremely talented, but if your values aren't
aligned, it will become a serious problem sooner or later. Conflicts can arise
around vision, commitment, accountability, and more.

Second, a technical cofounder has to actually be good at tech. In most cases,
they'll take on the CTO role. While the team is small, that means handling
everything technology-related; as the team grows, the focus shifts toward
hiring, managing engineers, and making strategic technical decisions.

If you're a non-technical founder, you won't be able to assess their skills
yourself the way a tech company would in an interview. So what can you do
instead? One option is to ask a friend in tech whom you trust to help evaluate.
Another smart move is to start with a smaller project together before
committing to anything bigger.

Here are a couple of high-level traits to look for in a technical cofounder:

**The ability to make sound decisions independently.**  Pay attention to how
they approach the project. If they ask for detailed specifications instead of
discussing the problem and proposing solutions, you may be dealing with
someone who lacks autonomy — not a good sign. And if they do make decisions
on their own but those decisions look strange, that's not a good sign either.

**A balance of technical enthusiasm and pragmatism.** It's natural for talented
developers to be passionate about technology; it's what they love, and it's why
they're good at it. But that passion is a double-edged sword. It can lead to
too much effort going into the technology rather than into the product itself,
and exotic technology choices can make onboarding new team members harder than
it needs to be. Your ideal technical cofounder should be able to strike that
tricky balance between shipping quickly and maintaining the quality and
security of the product.


## Technical cofounder: equity and salary

The most common equity split between two cofounders is 50/50, assuming both
joined at roughly the same time and are equally committed. A second founder
who comes on later — once the MVP is built and the startup has its first
customers — will typically get less, say 40/60. If they join significantly
later, the split may be 30/70 or narrower still. Founder shares should normally
vest over four years with a one-year cliff, which protects the company if
someone leaves early.

At the bootstrapped or pre-seed stage, founders often take no salary at all,
or just enough to cover living expenses. As the startup becomes profitable or
raises more funding, salaries rise, approaching market rates by Series A
and beyond.


## An alternative: hiring a CTO

A technical cofounder would typically take on the CTO role, but if you don't
have one, you can hire a CTO instead. If you offer little or no equity, though,
you'll need to make up for it with salary, and market rates for experienced
CTOs are high. Hiring someone less experienced is risky, since this person
will be responsible for many high-impact decisions, and getting those
decisions wrong can be expensive or even sink the startup.

A popular option for early-stage startups is a fractional CTO. It's the same
job as a full-time CTO, just part-time, so you get an experienced technology
leader for a fraction of the cost. Depending on where you look, the size of
your company, and the level of involvement, a fractional CTO can cost from
$3,000/month and up.

As always, there are pros and cons. Compared to working with a cofounder,
hiring a full-time or fractional CTO requires a budget, which can be a problem
when cash is tight. On the other hand, if you're not satisfied with their work,
parting ways with someone you hired is far simpler.

## Bottom line

Even in the age of AI, building a competitive technology startup still requires
technical skills. If you're lucky, a vibe-coded prototype might get you some
early traction, but scaling it while maintaining quality and security takes
real engineering discipline. So if you're not technical yourself, you'll most
likely need a technical cofounder or a CTO.

Finding the right person isn’t easy (though I’m not sure what’s even easy in
startups). When you're looking for a cofounder, the most important thing to
check is whether your values align. You'll be working together for a long time,
and serious conflicts between founders can sink a company. Beyond that, look
not just for excellent technical skills, but for a healthy balance between
enthusiasm for technology and pragmatism.
